## What it is

Once a line of fit is drawn, its equation is a model: put a value in and it predicts one out. The two numbers in y = mx + b each mean something real about the thing being measured.

**Key idea: In y = mx + b, m is the RATE — each extra unit of x adds m to y — and b is the predicted y when x is 0. Use the equation to predict in either direction.**

## How to solve it

1. Name the two numbers. In y = mx + b, m multiplies x and b stands on its own.
2. To predict y, put the x value in and work it out: multiply first, then add.
3. To interpret m, say it as a rate with BOTH units: "each extra 100 kg of payload adds 3.5 L/MIN". A bare number says nothing.
4. To interpret b, it is the predicted y when x is 0 — and check whether x = 0 even makes sense before you trust it. To predict x from y, subtract b then divide by m.

## Watch out for

- **Mistake:** swapping what m and b mean. **Fix:** m sits next to the x, so it is the one that changes as x changes. b never moves.
- **Mistake:** interpreting m as a bare number: "it goes up by 3.5". **Fix:** 3.5 what, per what? Without both units the sentence carries no information.

## Where it shows up

- **Briefing** — predicting fuel burn from payload weight.
- **Debrief** — predicting lock time from a pilot's hours.
- **Systems:** Fuel, Avionics.
- **Real aircraft:** a takeoff-distance chart is a linear model: each extra 1,000 lb of weight adds a fixed number of feet to the roll.
