## What it is

You almost never get to ask everybody. A sample is the part you do ask, and it is only useful if it looks like the whole group it came from.

**Key idea: A sample must be RANDOM and representative to support a conclusion. A convenience sample — whoever happened to be in the ready room — is biased.**

## How to solve it

1. Name the POPULATION: everyone or everything the question is about, not just the ones you can reach.
2. Name the SAMPLE: the part you actually collect data from.
3. Ask whether every member of the population had an equal chance of being picked. Only a random draw from the whole list does that.
4. Reject anything else. Easy to reach, already complaining, or all from one place are all biased, however many you ask.

## Watch out for

- **Mistake:** thinking a big sample cannot be biased. **Fix:** asking all 300 pilots at one base tells you about that base. Size does not fix the wrong group.
- **Mistake:** taking whoever is nearest. **Fix:** the pilots in the ready room are the ones not flying. That is already a particular kind of pilot.

## Where it shows up

- **Hangar** — a survey about a new helmet across the wing.
- **Debrief** — spot inspections across the fleet.
- **Systems:** Avionics, Weapons.
- **Real aircraft:** a fleet-wide airworthiness check samples tail numbers at random, precisely so no one base's maintenance habits skew the result.
