## What it is

A scatter plot puts two measurements on one pair of axes, one point per thing measured, so you can see whether they move together. A two-way table does the same job for two categories instead of two measurements.

**Key idea: On a scatter plot look for the trend (up, down, or nowhere), and for clusters and outliers. In a two-way table, a relative frequency is a row or column SHARE, not a count.**

## How to solve it

1. Read the scatter plot left to right. Points that climb are a POSITIVE association; points that fall are NEGATIVE; a shapeless cloud is NONE.
2. Look for points sitting away from the crowd. One of those is an outlier; a group of them is a cluster.
3. In a two-way table, every row adds to its row total and every column to its column total. A missing cell is a total minus the cells you can see.
4. A relative frequency is a SHARE: divide the cell by its row or column total, then multiply by 100 for a percent.

## Watch out for

- **Mistake:** naming the association backwards. **Fix:** follow the points from left to right. If they end up higher than they started, it is positive.
- **Mistake:** answering with the count when the question asked for a percent. **Fix:** "what percent" means divide by the total first. 120 out of 200 is 60%, not 120.

## Where it shows up

- **Sortie HUD** — altitude against speed on the radar tape.
- **Hangar** — faults by aircraft type and by system.
- **Systems:** Avionics, Engines.
- **Real aircraft:** maintenance data is usually a two-way table of aircraft type against fault type, and the useful figure is the share, not the raw count.
