## What it is

Now put the two ideas together: you are comparing two populations using only a sample from each. That means reading the plots AND saying how sure you are.

**Key idea: Compare centres and spreads, then judge your confidence. Boxes that sit clear of each other support a conclusion; overlapping boxes do not.**

## How to solve it

1. Read a centre off each plot. The line inside the box is that sample's median.
2. Read the spread of each: the box is the middle half, the whiskers reach the smallest and largest values.
3. Ask whether the boxes overlap. Clear of each other supports a conclusion; sitting on top of each other does not.
4. Say how confident you are. One sample from each is a hint, not a proof, and heavy overlap means the honest answer is to collect more.

## Watch out for

- **Mistake:** treating one sample from each group as proof. **Fix:** say "this sample came out higher", not "this squadron is faster". Another sample might not.
- **Mistake:** reading a clear result out of boxes that overlap heavily. **Fix:** if half of one box sits inside the other, the difference could easily be the luck of the draw.

## Where it shows up

- **Debrief** — do F-16 pilots or F-15 pilots lock faster, sampled at two bases.
- **Hangar** — comparing two maintenance schedules.
- **Systems:** Avionics, Engines.
- **Real aircraft:** a fly-off between two airframes is judged on samples of sorties, which is why the reports always quote the spread as well as the average.
