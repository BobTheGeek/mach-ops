## What it is

Before you can work out a probability for a compound event, you need the sample space itself: every outcome, listed once each. A table, a tree or an organised list all do the job.

**Key idea: Build the sample space with an organised list, a table or a tree, then count the outcomes that match the event as it is written in words.**

## How to solve it

1. Build the space in an organised way: a table for two devices, a tree for three. Organised means you can SEE that nothing is missing.
2. Count the outcomes by MULTIPLYING the sizes. Two dice of four faces give 4 × 4 = 16 cells.
3. Remember that order matters. (1, 2) and (2, 1) are two different outcomes and the table gives each its own cell.
4. Read the phrase carefully before you count. "At least one" includes the case where both happen; "exactly one" does not.

## Watch out for

- **Mistake:** counting (1, 2) and (2, 1) as one outcome, which halves the answer. **Fix:** the two dice are different dice. Both cells exist in the table.
- **Mistake:** reading "at least one 3" as "exactly one 3". **Fix:** "at least one" also counts the double 3. Underline the phrase before you start ticking cells.

## Where it shows up

- **Hangar** — how many loadout combinations exist.
- **Briefing** — the tree of mission outcomes.
- **Systems:** Weapons, Avionics.
- **Real aircraft:** a two-seat aircraft with four crew to choose from has 4 × 3 = 12 crew pairings, because front seat and back seat are different jobs.
